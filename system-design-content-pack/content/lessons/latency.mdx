## Three words that get mixed up

- **Latency**: how long one operation takes (ms).
- **Throughput**: work done per second (requests/s, MB/s).
- **Bandwidth**: a link's maximum capacity.

<Callout type="analogy" title="The highway">

Latency is one car's trip time; throughput is cars per hour. More lanes raise throughput, not speed.

</Callout>

Latency comes from distance, network hops, queuing and processing. <mark>Near maximum throughput, queues form and latency shoots up.</mark>

## The bottleneck sets the pace

A system is only as fast as its slowest part: a six-lane road feeding a one-lane bridge moves one lane of traffic.

## Report percentiles, not averages

Averages hide the slow requests users actually feel.

| Metric | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| p50 (median) | Half the requests are faster than this |
| p90 / p95 | 90% / 95% are faster |
| p99 | 99% are faster; the slowest 1% is the **tail** |
| p99.9 | Only 1 request in 1,000 is slower |

A p50 of 1 s with a p99 of 5 s means most users are fine, but 1 in 100 waits over 5 seconds.

The tail matters more at scale (*"the tail at scale"*). A page that calls 100 backends in parallel waits for the slowest one, so about <mark>63% of page loads (1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰)</mark> hit at least one backend's p99.

## Little's Law

It links throughput and latency: **requests in flight = throughput × average latency**.

At 5,000 req/s and 200 ms, about **1,000 requests** are inside the system at any moment. Each holds a thread, memory and often a DB connection. Use the law to size thread pools and connection pools. Plugging in p99 instead of the average gives a pessimistic upper bound.

## Latency numbers every engineer should know

These come from Peter Norvig's list, popularised by Jeff Dean. Exact values drift with hardware; <mark>the ratios are what drive design</mark>.

<Interactive name="latency-scale" />

| Compare | Gap | What it explains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RAM vs SSD random read | \~1,000–1,500× (100 ns vs 150 µs) | Why hot data is cached in RAM |
| Data-center round trip vs crossing a continent | \~300× | One cross-region call eats most of a 200 ms budget → CDNs, geo-replication |
| Disk seek vs reading 1 MB sequentially | One seek ≈ half a sequential megabyte | Sequential I/O wins → append-only logs (Kafka), LSM trees |
| Compress 1 KB vs send 2 KB over 1 Gbps | \~7× | Compress before sending over the network |

<Callout type="warning" title="Common slips">

Compressing 1 KB takes about **3 µs**, not 3 ns. "The same data center" means one building or campus; two cities are two data centers. And the classic 150 µs SSD figure predates NVMe drives, which read in tens of microseconds; RAM is still hundreds of times faster, which is the point.

</Callout>
